\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Site-specific management zones allow agricultural inputs to be prescribed
at rates adapted to the spatial characteristics of a field. The
Intra-Zone Homogeneity Delineation Problem (IHDP) partitions a field into
a minimum number of contiguous zones satisfying a prescribed global
relative-variance criterion, assuming that the soil attribute of every
geographic unit is known \citep{VicencioMedinaIHDP}. In practical nutrient
management, however, soil composition is only partially observed and
additional measurements are costly. The optimal locations at which to
acquire information, the resulting management zones, and the nutrient
application rates are therefore interdependent decisions.

This paper develops a \emph{probing-enhanced stochastic} extension in which
soil tests are selected before delineating a prescribed number of zones.
Upon observing their outcomes, the decision-maker constructs contiguous
management zones and selects a common nutrient application rate per zone.
The common objective is to minimize soil-testing and expected nutrient
application costs while meeting probabilistic crop-nutrition requirements.
We first formulate a \emph{core model} without an explicit homogeneity
restriction, so that the operational effect of delineation can be isolated.
We then introduce three alternative stochastic homogeneity specifications:
(i) a conditional expectation of the original IHDP relative-variance index,
(ii) a conditional chance constraint on that same index, and (iii) a bound
on the conditional expected variance within each individual zone. These
alternatives produce distinct optimization models that can be compared
under a common information and operational structure.

\section{Problem definition and assumptions}
\label{sec:problem}

Let $F$ be an agricultural field partitioned into $n$ geographic units
$\J=\{1,\ldots,n\}$, where unit $j$ has area $a_j>0$. A one-hectare grid
is represented by $a_j=1$. The undirected graph $G=(\J,\Eset)$ records
geographic adjacency: $\{j,k\}\in\Eset$ when two units share a boundary
segment. Let $\A=\{(j,k),(k,j):\{j,k\}\in\Eset\}$ be its directed
adjacency arcs. We assume $G$ is connected. The field must be divided
into exactly $K$ nonempty, contiguous zones, indexed by
$\I=\{1,\ldots,K\}$, where $1\le K<n$ is fixed in advance.

One nutrient and a predetermined crop are considered. The initial
plant-available nutrient in unit $j$ is the random quantity $\xi_j$,
measured in effective nutrient units per hectare. The known parameter
$\tau_j$ is the nutrient level required to attain the stipulated crop
performance in that unit; $\tau_j=\tau$ can be used for a spatially
uniform crop requirement. A zone receives a common effective application
rate, so the post-application nutrient level in unit $j$ is $\xi_j+d_j$.
This additive balance abstracts from heterogeneous uptake efficiencies.
The crop response is represented through a reliability requirement on
$\tau_j$, rather than through an explicitly calibrated yield function.

\subsection{Uncertainty, probing, and decision timing}
\label{subsec:information}

Let $\boldsymbol{\xi}=(\xi_j)_{j\in\J}$ be the vector of initial nutrient
levels, and $\boldsymbol{\eta}=(\eta_j)_{j\in\J}$ the vector of
\emph{potential} soil-test outcomes. A joint distribution
$P_{\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\eta}}$ is assumed known, including
spatial correlations. Testing unit $j$ may reveal its nutrient level exactly
($\eta_j=\xi_j$), or provide an imperfect measurement. The joint law is
assumed to admit regular conditional distributions, and the conditional
quantities used below are well-defined.

In the first stage, $z_j\in\{0,1\}$ indicates whether unit $j$ is tested;
thus the chosen testing set is
\begin{equation}
 S(z)=\{j\in\J:z_j=1\}.
 \label{eq:selected-set}
\end{equation}
Once the tests have been performed, only
$\boldsymbol{\eta}_{S(z)}=h$ is observed. In the second stage, the
partition and nutrient rates are selected as functions of $h$.
The true nutrient vector is not assumed to become fully observable before
these decisions, and there is no subsequent corrective action. All
second-stage decisions must consequently be measurable with respect to
$\sigma(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{S(z)})$. Constraints involving posterior
probabilities or expectations are required for almost every realized
observation $h$ under the selected testing design.

\subsection{Conditional nutrient sufficiency}
\label{subsec:quantile}

For each geographic unit $j$ and testing result $(S,h)$, define the
posterior lower-tail threshold
\begin{equation}
 q_{\epsilon,j}(S,h)
 :=\sup\left\{t\in\mathbb R:
 \PP(\xi_j<t\mid\boldsymbol{\eta}_S=h)\leq\epsilon\right\}.
 \label{eq:quantile-definition}
\end{equation}
We assume this threshold is finite. With this convention, which also
handles posterior distributions with atoms, the nutritional chance
constraint is equivalent to a deterministic lower bound on the rate:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 \PP\bigl(\xi_j+d_j\geq\tau_j\mid
     \boldsymbol{\eta}_S=h\bigr)\geq1-\epsilon
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 d_j\geq\tau_j-q_{\epsilon,j}(S,h).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:chance-equivalence}
\end{equation}
The guarantee is imposed \emph{separately} for each unit; it is not a
joint chance constraint on all geographic units simultaneously.

\section{Core probing-enhanced delineation and nutrient model}
\label{sec:core}

We first formulate the problem with probing, contiguous partitioning,
and uniform nutrient application, \emph{without} imposing a homogeneity
criterion. This core model provides the common mathematical structure
for all three alternative homogeneity specifications introduced in
Section~\ref{sec:homogeneity-alternatives}.

\subsection{Second-stage variables and connectivity representation}
\label{subsec:decisions}

For a fixed testing set $S$ and observation $h$, define:
\begin{itemize}
\item $x_{ji}\in\{0,1\}$: one if geographic unit $j$ is assigned to zone $i$;
\item $r_{ji}\in\{0,1\}$: one if $j$ is designated as the root of zone $i$;
\item $u_i\in[0,\bar u]$: common nutrient application rate for zone $i$;
\item $d_j\in[0,\bar u]$: application rate actually received by unit $j$;
\item $g_{ij}\geq0$: artificial source flow delivered to $j$ for zone $i$;
\item $f_{ijk}\geq0$: artificial flow on arc $(j,k)\in\A$ for zone $i$.
\end{itemize}
The flow variables enforce contiguity and do not describe the physical
transportation of nutrients. Each zone will have exactly one root, from
which a unit of artificial flow must reach each assigned geographic unit.

\subsection{Two-stage objective and core recourse problem}
\label{subsec:core-model}

The \emph{core} probing-enhanced model is
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\displaystyle
 (\mathrm{PE}\text{-}0)\qquad
 \min_{z\in\{0,1\}^{n}}
 \left\{
   \sum_{j\in\J}c_j^P z_j
   +\EE_{\boldsymbol{\eta}_{S(z)}}\left[
      Q_0\bigl(S(z),\boldsymbol{\eta}_{S(z)}\bigr)
    \right]
 \right\}.}
\label{eq:core-first-stage}
\end{equation}
Here the first term is the testing cost and $Q_0(S,h)$ denotes the
minimum second-stage nutrient cost after observing $h$. For fixed $(S,h)$,
its explicit formulation is
\allowdisplaybreaks
\begin{align}
Q_0(S,h)=\min_{x,r,g,f,u,d}\quad
 &c^N\sum_{j\in\J}a_jd_j
 &&\label{eq:recourse-objective}\\
\text{s.t.}\quad
 &\sum_{i\in\I}x_{ji}=1
 &&\forall j\in\J,\label{eq:assign}\\
 &\sum_{j\in\J}x_{ji}\geq1
 &&\forall i\in\I,\label{eq:nonempty}\\
 &\sum_{j\in\J}r_{ji}=1
 &&\forall i\in\I,\label{eq:one-root}\\
 &r_{ji}\leq x_{ji}
 &&\forall i\in\I,\ j\in\J,\label{eq:root-located}\\
 &\sum_{j\in\J}g_{ij}=\sum_{j\in\J}x_{ji}
 &&\forall i\in\I,\label{eq:source-balance}\\
 &0\leq g_{ij}\leq n\,r_{ji}
 &&\forall i\in\I,\ j\in\J,\label{eq:source-capacity}\\
 &\sum_{k:(k,j)\in\A}f_{ikj}+g_{ij}
       -\sum_{k:(j,k)\in\A}f_{ijk}=x_{ji}
 &&\forall i\in\I,\ j\in\J,\label{eq:flow-balance}\\
 &0\leq f_{ijk}\leq(n-1)x_{ji}
 &&\forall i\in\I,\ (j,k)\in\A,\label{eq:flow-tail}\\
 &f_{ijk}\leq(n-1)x_{ki}
 &&\forall i\in\I,\ (j,k)\in\A,\label{eq:flow-head}\\
 &d_j-u_i\leq\bar u(1-x_{ji})
 &&\forall i\in\I,\ j\in\J,\label{eq:dose-link-1}\\
 &u_i-d_j\leq\bar u(1-x_{ji})
 &&\forall i\in\I,\ j\in\J,\label{eq:dose-link-2}\\
 &d_j\geq\tau_j-q_{\epsilon,j}(S,h)
 &&\forall j\in\J,\label{eq:nutrition}\\
 &x_{ji},r_{ji}\in\{0,1\}
 &&\forall i\in\I,\ j\in\J,\label{eq:binary-domain}\\
 &f_{ijk},g_{ij}\geq0
 &&\forall i\in\I,\ (j,k)\in\A,\ j\in\J,\label{eq:flow-domain}\\
 &0\leq u_i\leq\bar u,\quad0\leq d_j\leq\bar u
 &&\forall i\in\I,\ j\in\J.\label{eq:dose-domain}
\end{align}
Let $\mathcal F_0(S,h)$ denote the feasible set described by
\eqref{eq:assign}--\eqref{eq:dose-domain}, and set $Q_0(S,h)=+\infty$
if this set is empty. The upper bound $\bar u$ is assumed sufficiently
large for the required nutrition rates whenever the model is intended
to have feasible recourse. The testing set $S=\varnothing$ is allowed.

\subsection{Interpretation of the core constraints}
\label{subsec:core-interpretation}

Objective \eqref{eq:core-first-stage} minimizes probing costs plus
expected nutrient expenditures, whereas
\eqref{eq:recourse-objective} computes the application cost for a
realized information state. Constraint \eqref{eq:assign} assigns every
unit to one zone and \eqref{eq:nonempty} ensures that all $K$ zone
labels are used. Constraints \eqref{eq:one-root}--\eqref{eq:root-located}
select one root in each zone.

Constraints \eqref{eq:source-balance}--\eqref{eq:flow-head} enforce
spatial contiguity through a single-commodity artificial flow for each
zone. Only the root can receive flow directly from the source; every
selected unit consumes one unit of flow; and flow on a geographic arc
is allowed only when both endpoints belong to the corresponding zone.
It follows that all assigned units are connected to the root through
nodes assigned to that zone, so the induced zone is connected.

Constraints \eqref{eq:dose-link-1}--\eqref{eq:dose-link-2} impose
$d_j=u_i$ whenever $x_{ji}=1$. Because both rates lie in
$[0,\bar u]$, these inequalities are inactive when $x_{ji}=0$.
Constraint \eqref{eq:nutrition} is the deterministic equivalent of
the unit-specific conditional nutritional chance constraint
\eqref{eq:chance-equivalence}. Finally,
\eqref{eq:binary-domain}--\eqref{eq:dose-domain} declare the variable
domains. The core model contains no explicit constraint requiring a
statistically homogeneous partition: instead, the uniform nutrient
rate makes heterogeneous zone assignments potentially expensive.

\section{Alternative stochastic homogeneity specifications}
\label{sec:homogeneity-alternatives}

We now add three \emph{alternative}, rather than simultaneous,
homogeneity requirements to the same core model. The first two retain
the global \emph{relative variance} (RV) measure of the original IHDP
\citep{VicencioMedinaIHDP}. The third preserves the conditional
within-zone variance criterion used in the initial version of our
probing-enhanced model. All three restrictions concern the \emph{initial}
soil nutrient field, before fertilizer is added.

\subsection{Relative variance of the original IHDP}
\label{subsec:rv-definition}

For a fixed assignment $x$, define the number of units and the realized
mean nutrient level of zone $i$ by
\begin{equation}
 \rho_i(x)=\sum_{j\in\J}x_{ji},\qquad
 \bar\xi_i(x,\boldsymbol\xi)
   =\frac{\sum_{j\in\J}x_{ji}\xi_j}{\rho_i(x)}.
 \label{eq:zone-mean}
\end{equation}
The pooled within-zone sample variance and the sample variance of the
entire field are, respectively,
\begin{align}
 \sigma_W^2(x,\boldsymbol\xi)
   &=\frac{1}{n-K}\sum_{i\in\I}\sum_{j\in\J}
        x_{ji}\bigl(\xi_j-\bar\xi_i(x,\boldsymbol\xi)\bigr)^2,
    \label{eq:pooled-variance}\\
 \sigma_F^2(\boldsymbol\xi)
   &=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{j\in\J}
       \bigl(\xi_j-\bar\xi_F(\boldsymbol\xi)\bigr)^2,
    \qquad
 \bar\xi_F(\boldsymbol\xi)=\frac1n\sum_{j\in\J}\xi_j.
    \label{eq:field-variance}
\end{align}
Expression \eqref{eq:pooled-variance} is identical to the pooled
sample-variance formula
$\sum_i(\rho_i-1)\sigma_i^2/(n-K)$ used in the IHDP;
for a singleton zone the corresponding sum of squared deviations is
zero. The relative variance of the realized nutrient field is
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\displaystyle
 RV(x,\boldsymbol\xi)
    =1-\frac{\sigma_W^2(x,\boldsymbol\xi)}
             {\sigma_F^2(\boldsymbol\xi)}.}
 \label{eq:rv-random}
\end{equation}
We assume $\PP(\sigma_F^2(\boldsymbol\xi)>0)=1$, so that
\eqref{eq:rv-random} is well-defined. The deterministic IHDP requires
$RV(x,\boldsymbol\xi)\geq\alpha$ when the soil attributes are known.
Here $\boldsymbol\xi$ is random, requiring a specification of what it
means to satisfy the threshold after partial information acquisition.

\subsection{Alternative H1: conditional expected relative variance}
\label{subsec:homogeneity-h1}

The first stochastic generalization of the original RV threshold is
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\displaystyle
 \EE\bigl[RV(x,\boldsymbol\xi)
          \mid\boldsymbol\eta_S=h\bigr]\geq\alpha.}
 \tag{H1}\label{eq:H1}
\end{equation}
This constraint requires a sufficiently high \emph{posterior expected}
RV for the whole partition; it does not impose a minimum RV in every
possible realization of the unknown nutrient field.

For a computationally useful equivalent expression, define
\begin{equation}
 B_{jk}(S,h)
 :=\EE\left[
  \frac{(\xi_j-\xi_k)^2}{\sigma_F^2(\boldsymbol\xi)}
    \;\middle|\;\boldsymbol\eta_S=h\right].
 \label{eq:posterior-normalized-diff}
\end{equation}
Using the pairwise identity
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{j\in\J}x_{ji}(\xi_j-\bar\xi_i)^2
 =\frac{1}{2\rho_i(x)}
    \sum_{j\in\J}\sum_{k\in\J}
     x_{ji}x_{ki}(\xi_j-\xi_k)^2,
 \label{eq:pairwise-identity}
\end{equation}
constraint \eqref{eq:H1} is \emph{exactly} equivalent to
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\displaystyle
 \sum_{i\in\I}\frac{1}{2\rho_i(x)}
    \sum_{j,k\in\J} B_{jk}(S,h)x_{ji}x_{ki}
       \leq(n-K)(1-\alpha).}
 \label{eq:H1-pairwise}
\end{equation}
Importantly, $B_{jk}(S,h)$ contains the \emph{expectation of a ratio},
not the ratio of two expected squared differences. In general,
\begin{equation}
 \EE\left[\frac{\sigma_W^2}{\sigma_F^2}\;\middle|\;h\right]
 \ne
 \frac{\EE[\sigma_W^2\mid h]}{\EE[\sigma_F^2\mid h]}.
 \label{eq:expectation-ratio}
\end{equation}
Consequently, substituting posterior expected variances into the
deterministic RV formula would give a different homogeneity measure.

\subsection{Alternative H2: conditional chance constraint on relative variance}
\label{subsec:homogeneity-h2}

The second extension retains the same realized RV but requires the
original threshold to hold with a prescribed posterior probability:
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\displaystyle
 \PP\bigl(RV(x,\boldsymbol\xi)\geq\alpha
        \mid\boldsymbol\eta_S=h\bigr)\geq1-\beta.}
 \tag{H2}\label{eq:H2}
\end{equation}
For instance, $\beta=0.05$ requires at least $95\%$ conditional
probability that the \emph{global partition} achieves the original IHDP
RV threshold. Equivalently, the event inside the probability is
\begin{equation}
 \sigma_W^2(x,\boldsymbol\xi)
 \leq (1-\alpha)\sigma_F^2(\boldsymbol\xi).
 \label{eq:H2-event}
\end{equation}
The probability in \eqref{eq:H2} is evaluated jointly over the
remaining uncertain soil composition; correlations between units are
therefore relevant. Unlike (H1), (H2) provides an explicit reliability
requirement. Neither formulation generally implies the other for a
common choice of $\alpha$ and $\beta$.

\subsection{Alternative H3: conditional expected within-zone variance}
\label{subsec:homogeneity-h3}

The third alternative requires every zone to have limited expected
intra-zone dispersion, instead of imposing a global RV threshold. For
zone $i$, define the realized \emph{population-normalized} variance
\begin{equation}
 V_i(x,\boldsymbol\xi)
 =\frac1{\rho_i(x)}\sum_{j\in\J}
  x_{ji}\bigl(\xi_j-\bar\xi_i(x,\boldsymbol\xi)\bigr)^2.
 \label{eq:within-zone-var}
\end{equation}
The corresponding conditional homogeneity requirement is
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\displaystyle
 \EE\bigl[V_i(x,\boldsymbol\xi)
       \mid\boldsymbol\eta_S=h\bigr]\leq\delta^2
       \qquad\forall i\in\I.}
 \tag{H3}\label{eq:H3}
\end{equation}
To obtain an equivalent expression, define
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 D_{jk}(S,h)
 &:=\EE\bigl[(\xi_j-\xi_k)^2
           \mid\boldsymbol\eta_S=h\bigr]\\
 &=\bigl(\mu_j(S,h)-\mu_k(S,h)\bigr)^2
    +\Sigma_{jj}(S,h)+\Sigma_{kk}(S,h)
    -2\Sigma_{jk}(S,h),
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:posterior-diff}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
 \mu_j(S,h)=\EE[\xi_j\mid\boldsymbol\eta_S=h],\qquad
 \Sigma_{jk}(S,h)=\Cov(\xi_j,\xi_k\mid\boldsymbol\eta_S=h).
 \label{eq:posterior-moments}
\end{equation}
It follows from \eqref{eq:pairwise-identity} that
\begin{equation}
 \EE\bigl[V_i(x,\boldsymbol\xi)\mid\boldsymbol\eta_S=h\bigr]
 =\frac{1}{2\rho_i(x)^2}
    \sum_{j,k\in\J}D_{jk}(S,h)x_{ji}x_{ki}.
 \label{eq:conditional-zone-var}
\end{equation}
Thus, (H3) admits the equivalent form
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\displaystyle
 \sum_{j,k\in\J}D_{jk}(S,h)x_{ji}x_{ki}
 \leq2\delta^2\rho_i(x)^2
 \qquad\forall i\in\I.}
 \label{eq:H3-pairwise}
\end{equation}
The coefficients $D_{jk}(S,h)$ account for posterior mean differences,
residual variances, and spatial covariance. Unlike (H1) and (H2), (H3)
is \emph{not normalized} by whole-field variance and is imposed
\emph{zone by zone}. It controls expected dispersion, not a probability
of satisfying a realized dispersion limit. When geographic units have
unequal areas, an area-weighted variance would be a separate modeling
choice; here we use equal statistical weights to retain comparability
with the original grid-based IHDP.

\subsection{Four models sharing the same operational formulation}
\label{subsec:variants}

Let $\mathcal H_t(S,h)$, $t\in\{1,2,3\}$, be the set of assignments
satisfying (Ht), and let $\mathcal H_0(S,h)$ contain all assignments.
For each variant define
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 Q_t(S,h)=\min_{(x,r,g,f,u,d)}\quad
    &c^N\sum_{j\in\J}a_jd_j\\
 \text{s.t.}\quad &(x,r,g,f,u,d)\in\mathcal F_0(S,h),\\
                 &x\in\mathcal H_t(S,h),
 \qquad t\in\{0,1,2,3\},
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:recourse-variants}
\end{equation}
with value $+\infty$ when infeasible. The complete probing-enhanced
model associated with alternative $t$ is
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\displaystyle
 (\mathrm{PE}\text{-}t)\qquad
 \min_{z\in\{0,1\}^{n}}
 \left\{\sum_{j\in\J}c_j^P z_j
 +\EE_{\boldsymbol\eta_{S(z)}}\left[
     Q_t(S(z),\boldsymbol\eta_{S(z)})\right]\right\},
 \quad t\in\{0,1,2,3\}.}
 \label{eq:four-models}
\end{equation}
All four variants employ the same information structure, objective,
connectivity representation, uniform dosing, and crop-nutrition
reliability constraint. Only the optional homogeneity requirement differs.

None of (H1)--(H3) is, in general, equivalent to another. In
particular, $\alpha$ is a dimensionless \emph{global} threshold, whereas
$\delta$ is an absolute nutrient-unit threshold on \emph{individual}
zones. These parameters should be calibrated separately; identical
numerical values do not correspond to comparable stringency. Because a
fixed $K$ may make any of (H1)--(H3) infeasible for some observed
information states, \eqref{eq:four-models} implicitly restricts
first-stage decisions to those with feasible second-stage recourse for
almost every observation.

\section{Optimal nutrient rate for a fixed partition}
\label{sec:optimal-dose}

The optimal nutrient decision admits a closed-form characterization
that is valid for all four models. This is useful for interpreting
how delineation affects application costs and for developing future
specialized solution methods.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:optimal-dose}
Fix a testing set $S$, an observation $h$, and a partition
$\Z=\{Z_1,\ldots,Z_K\}$ that is contiguous and satisfies whichever
homogeneity criterion is selected. Suppose $c^N>0$, $a_j>0$,
and the nutritional requirements can be satisfied without exceeding
$\bar u$. The optimal application rate in zone $i$ is
\begin{equation}
 u_i^*(S,h,\Z)
 =\max\left\{0,\max_{j\in Z_i}
        \bigl(\tau_j-q_{\epsilon,j}(S,h)\bigr)\right\}.
 \label{eq:closed-form-dose}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For every $j\in Z_i$, constraints
\eqref{eq:dose-link-1}--\eqref{eq:dose-link-2} imply $d_j=u_i$.
The nutritional requirement \eqref{eq:nutrition} therefore imposes
$u_i\geq\tau_j-q_{\epsilon,j}(S,h)$ for every $j\in Z_i$,
while nonnegativity imposes $u_i\geq0$. The cost incurred in zone $i$
is $c^Nu_i\sum_{j\in Z_i}a_j$, which is strictly increasing in
$u_i$. Its smallest feasible value is consequently optimal.
\end{proof}

The corresponding nutrient cost for a fixed partition becomes
\begin{equation}
 C^N(\Z\mid S,h)
 =c^N\sum_{i\in\I}\left(\sum_{j\in Z_i}a_j\right)
  \max\left\{0,\max_{j\in Z_i}
       \bigl(\tau_j-q_{\epsilon,j}(S,h)\bigr)\right\}.
 \label{eq:partition-cost}
\end{equation}
A single nutrient-deficient unit can therefore determine the dose
received by the whole zone. The cost objective already rewards
partitions with similar nutrient requirements, although it does not
guarantee any statistical homogeneity threshold. Because all three
homogeneity specifications concern only the pre-application nutrient
field, they do not affect the validity of
Proposition~\ref{prop:optimal-dose} once a partition is fixed.

\section{Finite-scenario formulation and endogenous nonanticipativity}
\label{sec:scenario}

For numerical implementation, consider a finite joint distribution
represented by scenarios $\Om$. Each scenario $\omega\in\Om$ has
probability $\pi_\omega>0$, $\sum_{\omega\in\Om}\pi_\omega=1$,
and specifies the pair
$(\boldsymbol\xi^\omega,\boldsymbol\eta^\omega)$.
Second-stage decisions are represented by
$(x^\omega,r^\omega,g^\omega,f^\omega,u^\omega,d^\omega)$.
The testing variables $z$ remain common to every scenario.

For each pair $\omega,\nu\in\Om$, define
\begin{equation}
 \Delta_{\omega\nu}
  =\{j\in\J:\eta_j^\omega\ne\eta_j^\nu\},\qquad
 m_{\omega\nu}(z)=\sum_{j\in\Delta_{\omega\nu}}z_j.
 \label{eq:distinguishability}
\end{equation}
Thus, $m_{\omega\nu}(z)=0$ precisely when scenarios $\omega$ and
$\nu$ cannot be distinguished by the selected tests. Define the
posterior information class and its probability as
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal C_\omega(z)
  =\{\nu\in\Om:m_{\omega\nu}(z)=0\},\qquad
 P_\omega(z)=\sum_{\nu\in\mathcal C_\omega(z)}\pi_\nu.
 \label{eq:information-class}
\end{equation}
For a fixed $z$, the conditional probability of scenario
$\nu\in\mathcal C_\omega(z)$ is $\pi_\nu/P_\omega(z)$.
The conditional nutrient thresholds
$q_{\epsilon,j}^\omega(z)$ are obtained from these probabilities,
and the posterior pairwise coefficients are
\begin{align}
 D_{jk}^\omega(z)
 &=\frac{1}{P_\omega(z)}
   \sum_{\nu\in\mathcal C_\omega(z)}\pi_\nu
          (\xi_j^\nu-\xi_k^\nu)^2,
 \label{eq:D-scenario}\\
 B_{jk}^\omega(z)
 &=\frac{1}{P_\omega(z)}
   \sum_{\nu\in\mathcal C_\omega(z)}\pi_\nu
   \frac{(\xi_j^\nu-\xi_k^\nu)^2}
        {\sigma_F^2(\boldsymbol\xi^\nu)}.
 \label{eq:B-scenario}
\end{align}
We assume $\sigma_F^2(\boldsymbol\xi^\nu)>0$ for every
scenario of positive probability.

\subsection{Common finite-scenario model}
\label{subsec:finite-core}

All four finite-scenario models minimize
\begin{equation}
 \min\quad
  \sum_{j\in\J}c_j^Pz_j
  +c^N\sum_{\omega\in\Om}\pi_\omega
           \sum_{j\in\J}a_jd_j^\omega,
 \label{eq:scenario-objective}
\end{equation}
subject to one copy of the core recourse constraints
\eqref{eq:assign}--\eqref{eq:dose-domain} for each $\omega\in\Om$,
with $q_{\epsilon,j}^\omega(z)$ in the nutrition constraint, plus
\emph{probing-dependent nonanticipativity}:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 |x_{ji}^\omega-x_{ji}^\nu|
 &\leq m_{\omega\nu}(z)
 &&\forall\omega,\nu\in\Om,\ j\in\J,\ i\in\I,\\
 |u_i^\omega-u_i^\nu|
 &\leq\bar u\,m_{\omega\nu}(z)
 &&\forall\omega,\nu\in\Om,\ i\in\I.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:NA}
\end{equation}
When $m_{\omega\nu}(z)=0$, indistinguishable scenarios yield
the same partition and zone nutrient rates. If a selected test
distinguishes the scenarios, the decisions may differ. The
absolute-value inequalities in \eqref{eq:NA} admit standard linear
representations. Artificial connectivity certificates need not be
nonanticipative because identical partitions admit a common feasible
certificate; the physical rates $d_j$ are already determined by the
common $x$ and $u$ decisions.

\subsection{Scenario counterparts of the three homogeneity variants}
\label{subsec:finite-homogeneity}

The following restrictions are imposed for each information class,
represented equivalently by every $\omega\in\Om$. Their form makes
explicit that the conditional distribution used to assess homogeneity
is \emph{endogenous} to the selected tests $z$.

\paragraph{PE-1: expected RV (H1).}
The finite-scenario counterpart of \eqref{eq:H1} is
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{\nu\in\mathcal C_\omega(z)}
    \pi_\nu\,RV(x^\omega,\boldsymbol\xi^\nu)
 \geq\alpha P_\omega(z)
 \qquad\forall\omega\in\Om.
 \label{eq:H1-scenario}
\end{equation}
An equivalent expression uses the normalized posterior coefficients
$B_{jk}^\omega(z)$ of \eqref{eq:B-scenario} in the pairwise constraint
\eqref{eq:H1-pairwise}.

\paragraph{PE-2: chance-constrained RV (H2).}
The finite-scenario counterpart of \eqref{eq:H2} is
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{\nu\in\mathcal C_\omega(z)}
  \pi_\nu\,\ind\!\left\{
       RV(x^\omega,\boldsymbol\xi^\nu)\geq\alpha
     \right\}
 \geq(1-\beta)P_\omega(z)
 \quad\forall\omega\in\Om.
 \label{eq:H2-scenario}
\end{equation}
The indicator denotes whether the same zone partition satisfies the
RV threshold under each remaining possible soil realization. It is a
mathematical representation of the event, not an assertion that the
constraint is already linear. Disjunctive or mixed-integer
reformulations would need additional modeling work.

\paragraph{PE-3: expected per-zone variance (H3).}
The finite-scenario counterpart of \eqref{eq:H3-pairwise} is
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{j,k\in\J}D_{jk}^\omega(z)
     x_{ji}^\omega x_{ki}^\omega
 \leq2\delta^2\left(\sum_{j\in\J}x_{ji}^\omega\right)^2
 \quad\forall\omega\in\Om,\ i\in\I.
 \label{eq:H3-scenario}
\end{equation}
No homogeneity constraints are added for PE-0. Thus, the four
scenario models differ only in their corresponding homogeneity
restriction and preserve the same information structure.

Although \eqref{eq:scenario-objective} and the core connectivity
constraints have mixed-integer linear structure, the posterior
coefficients in \eqref{eq:D-scenario}--\eqref{eq:B-scenario}, the
conditional nutrition quantiles, and the stochastic homogeneity
requirements depend on $z$. The resulting scenario formulations are
exact for a specified \emph{finite joint distribution}, but are not
claimed to be MILP models without further reformulation. For
continuous testing signals, an independently sampled finite set of
almost surely distinct observations can spuriously identify the
entire sampled scenario; meaningful implementation requires a suitable
signal discretization or a conditional-distribution approximation.

\section{Discussion and comparative evaluation}
\label{sec:discussion}

The family PE-0--PE-3 enables a controlled evaluation of whether
explicit statistical homogeneity requirements improve nutrient
management beyond the incentive already created by uniform
zone-level application. All models use the same $K$, nutrient costs,
probing technology, distribution of $(\boldsymbol\xi,\boldsymbol\eta)$,
and nutrition reliability level $1-\epsilon$. In particular, PE-0
isolates the benefit of cost-driven delineation without a separate
homogeneity target.

Comparisons should report not only expected total costs and the
selected tests, but also fertilizer expenditure, zone sizes and
spatial shapes, realized global RV, realized zone-level variances, and
out-of-sample frequencies of attaining both the nutrition and
homogeneity targets. The values of $\alpha$ and $\beta$ govern the
\emph{global} RV models, whereas $\delta$ has physical nutrient units
and acts separately on each zone. A sensitivity analysis or an
explicit calibration protocol is therefore needed before interpreting
performance differences as effects of modeling choice rather than
merely different constraint stringency.

A common nutrient rate does not change the within-zone variability
of initial nutrient availability under the assumed additive nutrient
balance, so delineation must identify compatible units before
application. Future research may consider multiple nutrients,
physical application constraints, variable-rate treatment within
zones, endogenous selection of $K$, sequential testing, and explicit
crop yield-response functions. The development of tractable exact
reformulations and tailored stochastic decomposition methods is left
for future work.
